# PER literature on lecture demonstrations (for PHY 210 / PHY 317 demo planning)

Compiled 2026-10-06. PER = physics education research. POE = predict-observe-explain.
ILD = Interactive Lecture Demonstration (Sokoloff & Thornton's 8-step protocol).
URL status: every URL below was fetched. "OK" = resolved and was the right page.
"403-bot" = publisher/site refuses scripted fetches but the DOI resolves to the
correct title (checked via the redirect target) -- fine in a browser.

Two caveats that apply to everything below:
- All controlled studies found are INTRODUCTORY courses (premeds, non-majors, high
  school). I found NO controlled study of demos in upper-division or math-methods
  courses. Applying these results to PHY 210/317 juniors is an inference, flagged
  where it matters.
- One trap commonly attributed to Miller et al. 2013 ("students misremember the
  outcome in the direction of their prediction") is NOT what that paper found. See
  ref `miller2013` and Protocol step 5.

## Protocol

A recipe for one demo inside a 75-min active-learning class. Minute budgets are for a
demo whose physical run is about 2 min. Brackets point to `refs` ids.

1. **Pose (1 min).** Show the apparatus and do everything EXCEPT the decisive part
   (ILD step 1). Name exactly what to watch ("which ball reaches the bottom first",
   "the phase of the shadow relative to the bob"). Roth et al. found students can't
   separate signal from noise unless told what the signal is. [sokoloff1997, roth1997]
2. **Predict individually, in writing, with a reason (1-2 min).** No talking. A
   written commitment is the single ingredient with the best gain-per-minute in the
   literature: +2 min for a jump from no-gain to a significant gain. [crouch2004,
   showthephysics]
3. **Vote (0.5 min).** Cards or clicker, 3-4 options built from known wrong answers.
   Show the distribution; do not reveal the answer or praise anyone. Crouch et al.
   note plausible distractors raise the in-class error rate -- that is fine, it is
   the point. [crouch2004, showthephysics]
4. **Peer discussion + revote (2-4 min, OPTIONAL).** Pays off when the demo was
   designed around a known difficulty (ILD-style); for an ordinary demo it added
   only 2 percentage points on explanations for 8 extra min. Use it when the vote
   is split (roughly 30-70% right), skip it when it is not. [crouch2004,
   zimrot2007, sokoloff1997]
5. **Observe, then each student writes what they SAW (2 min run + 0.5 min write).**
   Run it so the outcome is unambiguous (twice if cheap; close-up camera to the
   projector if the effect is small -- video beat live in a randomized trial because
   it directs attention). Then state the outcome in one plain sentence on the board.
   TRAP: about 1 in 5 students report the wrong outcome even right after watching.
   Older studies (Gunstone & White 1981; Champagne et al. 1985; Hynd et al. 1994,
   cited in Miller 2013) said students "see what they predicted"; Miller et al. found
   the misreport rate is about the same for right (16%) and wrong (20%) predictors,
   so the fix is not just for the wrong-predictors -- it is to make every student
   record the observed outcome and to say it out loud. [miller2013, kestin2020]
6. **Reconcile, students first (3-4 min).** Ask someone who predicted wrong why that
   answer was reasonable, then someone to explain the result. Do NOT slide back into
   lecture -- Sokoloff & Thornton list that as the top pitfall. Bridge
   representations explicitly (apparatus -> sketch -> equation): unbridged switches
   were one of Roth's six failure modes. [sokoloff2018nfw, roth1997]
7. **Transfer case (1-2 min).** One "same physics, different surface" question (ILD
   step 8) or the equation the demo just justified. Explanation scores stayed around
   30% even with prediction, so the demo must feed a following activity or
   derivation, not stand alone. [sokoloff1997, crouch2004, miller2013]
8. **Record (0.5 min, can be homework).** One line in notes: prediction, outcome,
   reason. Retention: students who observed correctly mostly still remembered at
   semester's end. [miller2013]

Total: about 9-11 min without step 4, 12-15 min with it.

Other traps the literature names:
- Demo with no prediction ~ no learning of explanations (observe mode h = 0.05,
  not significant). [crouch2004]
- A demo that merely confirms what was just lectured gives no conflict to resolve;
  conceptual change needs a demo built to contradict a known wrong idea. [zimrot2007,
  crouch2004 citing Kraus 1997]
- A demo works best when students already have a basic grasp of the concept
  (Miller: prior understanding predicts correct observation) -- so a demo is usually
  better AFTER a first pass at the idea than as the opening hook. [miller2013]
- Successive demos interfere; one demo per idea, not a parade. [roth1997]
- Complex "treasured" demos teach little unless broken into short predictable
  pieces. [sokoloff2018nfw]
- Do not grade predictions or praise right ones; it suppresses honest guesses.
  [showthephysics, sokoloff2018nfw]

## Time arithmetic

What the data actually measured (Crouch et al. 2004, Table I, 15-20-student sections
-- close to Smith class sizes, but intro premeds, not juniors):

| Mode    | Class time | Outcome correct | Explanation correct |
| ------- | ---------- | --------------- | ------------------- |
| no demo | 0 min      | 61%             | 22%                 |
| observe | 11 min     | 70%             | 24% (n.s.)          |
| predict | 13 min     | 77%             | 30%                 |
| discuss | 21 min     | 82%             | 32%                 |

(n.s. = not statistically significant vs no demo. "Class time" includes running
the demo and the instructor's explanation, not only the wrapper.)

**Minimum wrapper around a 2-minute demo.** Even "traditional" show-and-explain cost
11 min in that study, so a 2-min demo is never a 2-min item. The cheapest
configuration with a measured benefit is predict mode: pose + written prediction +
vote (about 3 min) before, and written observation + stated outcome + student-led
reconcile (about 4 min) after -- i.e. about 7 min of wrapper, 9 min total. Below
about 5 min of wrapper (no individual written prediction, or the instructor
explains instead of students reconciling) you are in observe mode, where the
explanation gain was indistinguishable from zero; at that point the demo is
entertainment (possibly still worth it for motivation, but not as instruction --
and the brief says not to stuff in filler). Inference, not measured: juniors may
move faster through pose/vote, but the reconcile step is where the learning is and
should not be squeezed.

**What a 15-20 minute demo must deliver.** 20 min is the price of Crouch's discuss
mode, which for ordinary demos bought only +2 points over a 13-min predict-mode
demo. A long demo justifies itself only if it does what the ILD/conflict studies
say produces the extra gain (Crouch notes discuss mode helped more with ILD-designed
demos; Zimrot & Ashkenazi):
1. it targets a specific, known wrong prediction (a split vote is expected);
2. it contains 2-3 predict-observe cycles, not one long show (ILD sequences run 5-7
   short demos per hour, so about 8-12 min per cycle);
3. it REPLACES class time the topic needed anyway -- the derivation, the worked
   example or the homework setup flows directly from it (e.g. the brachistochrone
   race motivates Euler-Lagrange, then the tautochrone surprise is the second
   cycle), rather than being added on top;
4. its result is reused later (homework, exam, the next class), since one-shot
   demos left about 70% unable to give a full explanation at semester's end.
If a long demo can't name items 1 and 3, cut it to a short predict-mode demo or to a
video (Kestin 2020: video with close-ups and slow motion out-taught live -- useful
for demos with long setup or small visual effects).

Upper-division gap (flagged): no PER study found measures any of this in junior-level
mechanics or math-methods courses; the numbers above are intro-course numbers and
the long-demo criteria are inferences from them.
